## Domain expert review: SNAP program and quality-control specialist

Reviewed: `/Users/maxghenis/PolicyEngine/snap-qc-sim/paper/index.qmd` (checked against `/Users/maxghenis/PolicyEngine/snap-qc-sim/paper/FACTS.md`, the FY2024 technical documentation at `/Users/maxghenis/.cache/axiom-oracles/snap-qc/techdoc.txt`, and the official FY2024 PER table at `/Users/maxghenis/.cache/axiom-oracles/snap-qc/snap-fy24QC-PER.pdf`).

### Recommendation: Major revisions

The quantitative core is unusually trustworthy — I verified the sampling formulas, the all-states-reduced-schedule claim, the tolerance history, the BENFIX/FSBEN usage, and every official state rate cited, and all check out against primary sources. The required revisions are about domain scoping, not arithmetic: the paper analyzes the QC *public-use file* as if it were the QC *system*, and the gap between the two (excluded ineligible cases, the edited file's enforced internal consistency, the unpriced negative-case system, the measurement-integrity history) touches several headline claims. All are fixable with added text, one reconciliation table, and one sensitivity note; none require rebuilding the pipeline.

---

### Major issues

**1. The public-use file excludes exactly the error cases that matter most, and the paper never says so.** Per the techdoc (lines 909–940 and Table II.1 at lines 941–1039), the edited file drops: cases with ineligible findings (STATUS = 4/5; 1,037 cases in FY2024, ~2.0% of completed reviews), cases eligible but with zero benefit due or with overissuance equal to or exceeding the issued benefit (STATUS = 2 and RAWBEN ≤ AMTERR; 406 cases), incomplete reviews (6,332), and inconsistency drops (84). Under the official methodology, an ineligible finding scores the *entire* issued benefit as error dollars (techdoc lines 779–780) — so the PUF is truncated precisely at the top of the error-magnitude distribution. Consequences the paper must address:
- The Colorado juxtaposition "the file-derived 8.88%; the official regression-adjusted rate is 9.97%" (index.qmd:213–214) invites the reader to attribute the 1.09-point gap to regression adjustment. A large share is plausibly the PUF's exclusion of ineligible and full-overissuance cases (nationally, $8.1B PUF error dollars vs. ~$10.1B implied by 10.93% of ~$92.8B issuance). Decompose or explicitly name the gap's components.
- The decomposition's "86.9% of error dollars are wrong facts" is estimated on *partial-error cases among eligible units only*. Ineligibility errors — the category the cost-share debate cares most about — are outside the universe. The "information failure, not calculation failure" conclusion very likely survives (ineligibility errors are overwhelmingly facts-driven, which would push the share up), but that should be argued, not left implicit.
- The simulator's bootstrap and the fitted deviation distribution both operate on a support missing its extreme mass points, so simulated sampling variance — the input to the "tier assignment is a coin flip" and the $33M SD numbers — is understated. Direction favors the paper's thesis (true flip risk is, if anything, larger), but the numbers as printed are biased and should carry a stated direction-of-bias note or a sensitivity run that re-adds an ineligible-case mass point per state.

**2. The parity target is Mathematica's edited, internally consistent computation chain — not 6,081 independent reviewer adjudications.** The techdoc is explicit that the editing process *enforces* the very identities the harness asserts: "The overall strategy of the editing process is to ensure that certain relationships hold for all cases," including net income = gross minus deductions and benefit = maximum benefit minus 30% of net income, with inconsistent units dropped (lines 1055–1071, 1836–1841). FSBEN is a constructed variable computed by the Minimodel formula (lines 9565–9584); the reviewer-adjudicated quantities are RAWBEN/BENFIX/AMTERR/STATUS, which the parity suite does not target. So zero mismatches across 36,486 cells is not 6,081 independent verifications of "reviewer arithmetic" (index.qmd:169, "reproduce the reviewer arithmetic exactly"); it certifies that the engine's parameter tables (standard deductions, maximum allotments, minimum benefit), rounding conventions, and computation order match the Minimodel's, over the observed diversity of case configurations. That is still a genuinely valuable test — the two defects it caught (stale dollar literal, missing rounding step) are exactly the class it can catch — but the abstract's "real, adjudicated benefit computations... carrying its inputs and its reviewed outputs" (index.qmd:57–59) and sec-oracle's "adjudicated by the agency's own reviewers" (index.qmd:138–139) overstate. The scope section (index.qmd:177–192) concedes supplied intermediates but not the enforced-consistency point. Fix: name the exact file variables asserted at each of the six stages, characterize the target as the edited-file/Minimodel chain, and move the reviewer-adjudicated fields explicitly to the decomposition sections where they are actually used.

**3. Negative cases and the one-sided pricing of error are absent.** The paper is careful to say "active cases" (index.qmd:49–50) and nowhere falsely claims negative-case coverage — good. But the QC system's other half (denials/terminations/suspensions reviewed for correctness, feeding CAPER; techdoc lines 734–736, 782–784) never appears, even though FACTS.md row A4 shows the authors know the negative-case sampling formulas. This is not a completeness nicety: OBBBA prices only the active-case PER. A state can lower its priced error rate by tightening the front door, shifting error into an unpriced metric — the same incentive asymmetry the paper analyzes for audit volume (index.qmd:363–374), and arguably the larger one. Relatedly, the paper never notes that the PER *sums* over- and underpayments (Colorado's 9.97 = 7.91 over + 2.06 under; Maryland's 13.64 includes a 4.79 underpayment rate — the nation's highest), so cost sharing partly bills states for underpaying their own residents. One paragraph in sec-qc plus a beat in the incentives discussion would fix both.

**4. The QC measurement-integrity history is missing, and it bears directly on the paper's premises.** Between roughly 2009 and 2016, multiple states used consultant-driven "error review committees" to bias QC findings downward; DOJ recovered over $32M in False Claims Act settlements (Texas $15M+, Florida $17.5M, Tennessee $6.85M, Mississippi $5M, Virginia $7.15M, Wisconsin $6.99M, Alaska $2.5M, plus the Osnes consultancy), FNS issued formal anti-bias guidance, and national rates for FY2015–16 were not published. This matters here three ways: (a) the model's training era begins in FY2017 precisely because earlier labels were compromised — currently unremarked; (b) the "error label is a property of the QC measurement system" caveat (index.qmd:421–424) has a concrete, documented failure mode, not a hypothetical one; (c) OBBBA multiplies the payoff to depressing measured rates by orders of magnitude, so the FY2025–27 files the paper says "will begin to answer" behavioral questions (index.qmd:428) are themselves endogenous to the incentive. The 2025 commentary already frames this ("states are gaming SNAP error rates"). A paper whose thesis is "this measurement system now sets prices" cannot omit the documented history of that system being manipulated when far less money was at stake.

**5. The delay-clause description drifts from the statute (and from the paper's own fact catalog).** FACTS.md A3 has it right: implementation is delayed to FY2029 for states whose *FY2025* rate × 1.5 ≥ 20%, and to FY2030 for states whose *FY2026* rate crosses the same test. The manuscript compresses this to "delayed... where 1.5 times **the keyed rate** reaches 20%" (index.qmd:38–41), where "the keyed rate" was just defined as the FY2025-or-2026 rate *at the state's election*. The delay trigger is mechanical on each specific year's rate and independent of the state's election. Also worth adding: the implied threshold is 13.33%, and on the FY2024 table roughly ten jurisdictions (AK, DC, FL, GA, MA, MD, NJ, NM, NY, OR) would qualify if FY2025 resembles FY2024 — which makes the footnote's projection point concrete. Tier boundary semantics ("reaches 6%", i.e., inclusive lower bounds) are correct as legislated; the election description matches the enrolled text and FNS implementation guidance.

**6. The heat-and-eat lever now runs against the statute that created the cost share.** OBBBA itself (§ 10103) restricts the LIHEAP-triggered HCSUA to households with an elderly or disabled member; states have already implemented this (e.g., California ACL 25-50, effective Nov 2025). The paper's four-lever accounting bound (index.qmd:376–388) is computed on the FY2024 error mix and presented as a forward-looking cost-share lever ($606M/$1.31B) without noting that the SUA-standardization channel was just narrowed by law — for non-elderly households the change moves in the opposite direction (more verification exposure, not less). One sentence in sec-simulate and one in limitations suffices; silence will read as unawareness to any FNS or state-agency reader.

### Minor issues

1. **"Stratified sample" (index.qmd:49) is wrong as a blanket description.** 7 CFR 275.11 requires a probability sample; the techdoc says states "may use either a simple random sampling plan or a more complex sampling design" with FNS approval (lines 842–845). Many states use systematic sampling. Say "probability sample." This also matters for the bootstrap: iid resampling ignores whatever design the state actually uses, and the official rate's variance additionally includes the federal-subsample/regression-adjustment component — worth one limitations clause.
2. **The reduced schedule's no-dispute condition is a missed gem, not just an omission.** A state may elect the smaller sample only "if it agrees not to dispute later payment error rate findings and the associated sanctions on the basis of the precision of the estimates" (techdoc lines 876–877). All 53 jurisdictions have so agreed — while the paper's central finding is that precision now determines tier placement worth tens of millions. Whether states revert to the standard schedule (regaining both precision and dispute rights) is a live FY2026 sampling-plan question that connects directly to the audit-volume section. Incorporate it; it strengthens the paper.
3. **"305 of 856 sampled cases carry payment errors" (index.qmd:211–212) conflicts with the paper's own definition discipline.** Sec-qc correctly insists the official label is thresholded; 305/856 = 35.6% against a 13.4% national weighted base rate strongly suggests the 305 (STATUS 2/3 per FACTS D1) includes sub-$56 deviations that are not payment errors under the official definition. Label them "recorded payment deviations" or report the thresholded count alongside.
4. **Anchoring description inconsistency.** The manuscript says the simulator "anchors each state's baseline at its official rate" (index.qmd:318–320, 349–350); FACTS.md E6 says the app "anchors levels on the model's own baseline mean." Reconcile — these are different conventions and the tool's methods note is presumably the ground truth.
5. **BENFIX/FSBEN usage is correct — codebook-consistent — but add one provenance clause.** BENFIX ("Benefit allotment adjusted for errors," constructed; techdoc 4947–4951) is Mathematica-constructed from the reviewer's findings, not a raw reviewer field; RAWBEN is "reported... certified to receive during the sample month" (9741–9758). Calling BENFIX "the reviewer-determined correct allotment" (index.qmd:100–102) is conceptually right; note the constructed origin. The choice of RAWBEN − BENFIX over FSBEN as deviation target — and the documented audit that forced it — is exactly the right domain call, and the 99.997% vs 83.65% concordance evidence is convincing.
6. **Layer 1 needs a coder-discretion caveat and code-level transparency.** The strict/broad classes map cleanly onto AGENCY codes 17/19/20 and +10/21/22 (techdoc 11812–11973); state the codes in the text or an appendix. Note that cause coding is performed by state QC reviewers under handbook guidance with known cross-state variation, that FY2024 brought "minor changes to detailed error findings variables, including AGENCY, ELEMENT, and NATURE codes" (lines 654–656) — relevant to pooling 2017–2023 features — and defend the classification edges (why code 10 "policy incorrectly applied" is in the broad computation class while 18 "data entry and/or coding error" is not). The three-layer triangulation already hedges this well; make the hedge explicit.
7. **SMD description: add the cost-neutrality offset.** The techdoc confirms SMD is demonstration-project authority, 25 states by end-FY2024, Kentucky new in Oct 2023, and — critically — "to achieve cost neutrality... most States reduced the HCSUA for the entire caseload" (lines 3213–3224). So SMD adoption mechanically perturbs the utility-allowance channel too: the adoption contrasts are contrast-of-bundles, and the SMD and heat-and-eat levers in the simulator are not independent. The contrast states used (AZ May 2023, CA Oct 2021, KY Oct 2023, per Table III.4) check out.
8. **Sample-size framing.** "Hundreds to a couple thousand active cases" (index.qmd:49–50) describes the regulatory range, but since every state elected the reduced schedule (verified: techdoc line 894), no state's minimum exceeds 1,020; realized PUF counts are lower still because of incompletes and exclusions. Also worth noting FNS's completion-rate adjustment (98% threshold, line 894–895) — national completion was 88% in FY2024 — as another unmodeled adjustment channel.
9. **Tolerance history is accurate** ($38 in FY2017, $56 in FY2024, from the $37 FY2014 statutory base, TFP-indexed — confirmed by the PER footnote and FNS QC memoranda). Consider citing FNA § 16(c)(1)(A)(ii) rather than leaving "indexed annually" uncited.
10. **Audit-buy economics.** Two cheap additions to sec-simulate: QC reviews cost on the order of low thousands of dollars each, so the Missouri/Tennessee expected savings imply enormous ROI; and OBBBA separately cut the federal administrative match from 50% to 25% (FY2027), doubling the state-side price of every additional review.
11. **Bibliography is thin on the domain.** Missing: the FNS QC Review Handbook (FNS 310-1) that defines the review procedures the entire label rests on; CRS's "Errors and Fraud in SNAP" (R45147/IF10860); the FNS/DOJ integrity materials (major issue 4); FRAC's 2025 QC/tolerance/cost-sharing backgrounder; USDA's OBBBA implementation memorandum (source for election mechanics); CBPP, AEI, and Urban 2025 analyses of the cost-share provision (positioning against the live debate); and a citation for SMD demonstration authority (FNA § 17(b)(1)). The FRAC SMD report appears to be in the project's source cache but not the bibliography.

### What a domain reviewer would still list as missing

- Federal re-review mechanics beyond one clause: subsample sizes, how the regression adjustment maps state findings to official rates, and the arbitration process by which states dispute federal findings (7 CFR 275.3) — all of which become financially decisive from FY2028.
- Any mention of state sampling plans in practice (states may sample above minima; oversampling/deselection; monthly allocation), which is the natural home for the no-dispute point above.
- The 2025 integrity discourse (major issue 4) and the composition question (over vs. under) in the incentives analysis.

### Strengths

- **Factual fidelity is exceptional for this literature.** Every checkable claim I tested against primary sources passed: the 275.11 formulas (300 / +0.042 to 2,400; optional 300 / +0.0153(N−12,941) to 1,020), "all States chose the optional minimum sample size" in FY2024 (techdoc line 894, verbatim support), $56 FY2024 and $38 FY2017 tolerances, and every cited official rate to the decimal (CO 9.97 = 7.91 + 2.06, ND 7.91, WA 6.06, KS 9.98, NV 5.94, NY 14.09, AK 24.66, national 10.93). Internal arithmetic is consistent throughout (e.g., the $63M tier step and $156M expectation cohere with the 47% flip probability).
- **The BENFIX-vs-FSBEN correction is the single most important data decision in the paper, made correctly and documented honestly** — including publishing the audit that caught the earlier wrong target. The techdoc's variable definitions fully support the paper's usage.
- **The three-layer decomposition (cause codes → natures → engine replay) is exactly how a QC veteran would triangulate**, and the layers' agreement (roughly 3–13% computation-side) is credible; the "wrong facts, correct arithmetic" headline matches what practitioners believe but have never seen demonstrated at case level with a verified engine.
- **The tier-lottery and two-sided audit-volume findings are genuinely novel, policy-relevant, and timely** — states are electing FY2025-vs-FY2026 rates now, and no one else has put sampling noise in dollar terms per state. The observation that above-boundary states are rewarded for preserving noise is the kind of institutional insight FNS and NAWRS audiences will quote.
- **Scope honesty is above field norms**: accounting bounds labeled as identities not causal claims, the New York transport failure featured rather than buried, the dissolved Kentucky estimate reported as a caution, deterministic byte-identical reruns, and a fact catalog (`paper/FACTS.md`) mapping claims to artifacts — including prohibitions on superseded numbers. The self-employment net-vs-gross data constraint is real and correctly stated (techdoc line 11557).
- The reported FNS techdoc errata, if upstreamed, are a public-good contribution in their own right.
